Before buying a camera for a telescope, or a telescope for a camera,
two questions decide whether the pair will work: how much sky does the
sensor cover, and how much sky does each pixel cover? The first decides
which targets fit. The second decides whether the camera can record the
detail the sky allows.

## Pixel scale

$$
\text{scale} = 206.265 \times \frac{p}{F}
$$

<Katex tex="p" /> is the pixel size in micrometres and <Katex tex="F" />
the focal length in millimetres; the result is in arcseconds per pixel.
206,265 is the number of arcseconds in a radian, and the micrometre and
millimetre units take care of the factor of 1,000. Binning 2×2 combines
four pixels into one, which doubles the scale.

A RedCat 51 (250 mm) with a 3.76 µm camera gives about 3.1″ per pixel.
The same camera on a C8 at 2032 mm gives 0.38″ per pixel, eight times
finer.

## Sampling: matching the camera to the sky

The atmosphere blurs every star into a small disc. Its width, the FWHM
(full width at half maximum), is your seeing: about 2″ on a good night
at a decent site, 3″ on an average one. The usual aim is 2 to 3 pixels
across that disc.

- **Undersampled (under 2 px):** stars look blocky and fine detail is
  lost. Common, and usually acceptable, with short refractors and
  camera lenses aimed at wide fields. Drizzle integration in stacking
  software can win some back.
- **Well sampled (2–3 px):** round stars, with no light wasted on extra
  pixels.
- **Oversampled (over 3 px):** the same detail spread over more pixels,
  each collecting less light, so the image is noisier for the same
  exposure time. Common with long-focal-length scopes. Binning, or a
  focal reducer, fixes it.

Planetary imaging is the exception. With very short exposures that
freeze the seeing ("lucky imaging"), planetary imagers deliberately
sample much finer, usually at a focal ratio around 5 times the pixel
size in µm.

## Field of view

$$
\text{FOV} = 2 \arctan\left(\frac{s}{2F}\right)
$$

<Katex tex="s" /> is the sensor's width or height in millimetres. The
framing preview draws the sensor and a target at the same scale, so
you can see whether the whole Andromeda Galaxy fits (it needs about
3° × 1°) or whether the Ring Nebula is a speck.

## Untracked exposures: the NPF and 500 rules

On a fixed tripod the sky turns during the exposure, and stars trail.
The old 500 rule divides 500 by the focal length times the crop factor.
It was worked out for film and allows visible trailing on today's
high-resolution sensors. The NPF rule, from Frédéric Michaud of the
Société Astronomique de France, takes the f-number and pixel size into
account:

$$
t = \frac{35N + 30p}{f}
$$

Both give the worst case, for stars on the celestial equator. Stars
near the celestial pole move less, so you can expose longer when
pointing there.
