Every time a charged particle speeds up, slows down or changes direction, it
gives off light. Bremsstrahlung, German for "braking radiation", is what
happens when the thing doing the pushing is another charge: an electron
flying past an atomic nucleus is pulled off course by its electric field, and
the swerve shakes loose a photon. It is how a hospital makes X-rays, and it is
how the hottest gas in the universe shines.

## One swerve, one photon

An accelerating charge radiates with a power set by how hard it is
accelerated, the Larmor formula:

$$
P = \frac{q^2 a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0 c^3}
$$

An electron passing a nucleus feels the strongest pull at its point of
closest approach, so that's where the light comes from. The first panel
follows one electron through. Its photon's energy depends on how sharp the
swing is: an encounter at distance <Katex tex="b" /> lasts about
<Katex tex="b/v" />, and a pulse that short carries photons of roughly

$$
E_\gamma \approx \frac{\hbar v}{b}
$$

A near miss makes a hard X-ray; a distant pass, a soft one. Heavier nuclei
pull harder: a nucleus with charge <Katex tex="Ze" /> bends the electron
<Katex tex="Z" /> times as much, and since the power goes as the square of the
acceleration, it shines <Katex tex="Z^2" /> times as bright. Tungsten
(<Katex tex="Z = 74" />) outshines hydrogen more than five thousand times.

The bend itself is usually small. An electron with a few tens of keV,
passing a hydrogen nucleus at a typical distance, is deflected by well under
a degree, so the animation exaggerates it. At these scales the electron is
really a quantum wave about a picometre across, and the photon only
sometimes appears, but the classical picture gets the essentials right.

## The X-ray tube

When Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895, he was looking at
bremsstrahlung from electrons crashing into the glass and metal of a
discharge tube. A modern X-ray tube is the same idea: electrons are
accelerated through a voltage <Katex tex="V" /> and slammed into a metal
target, usually tungsten. Each electron swerves past many nuclei as it slows
down, giving off a spray of photons with every energy up to its own. That
makes a smooth spectrum that falls in a straight line, Kramers' law:

$$
\frac{dI}{dE} \propto Z\,(eV - E)
$$

and it stops dead at <Katex tex="E = eV" />, when one electron hands over all
its energy at once. That edge, the Duane–Hunt limit, gives the shortest
wavelength a tube can make:

$$
\lambda_\text{min} = \frac{hc}{eV}
$$

It depends only on the voltage, not on the target, which is how William
Duane and Franklin Hunt used it in 1915 to measure Planck's constant.
Bremsstrahlung is a very inefficient way to make light: only about
<Katex tex="9\times10^{-10}\,ZV" /> of the electrons' energy comes out as
X-rays, under 1% even for tungsten at 100 kV. The rest heats the target,
which is why X-ray tubes use tungsten, with the highest melting point of any
metal, and spin the target to spread the heat.

The same inefficiency has a practical lesson: to block beta radiation, which
is fast electrons, you use plastic or aluminium first, not lead. Lead's
heavy nuclei would turn the electrons' energy into penetrating X-rays.

## When the whole gas glows

In a gas hot enough to strip electrons from their atoms, electrons are
constantly swinging past ions, and the gas glows with thermal bremsstrahlung,
also called free-free emission (the electron is free before and after). The
light from a gas at temperature <Katex tex="T" /> peaks near photon energy
<Katex tex="kT" /> and fades fast above it. The total power from each cubic
metre is

$$
\varepsilon \approx 1.4 \times 10^{-40}\,\sqrt{T}\,n_e\,n_i~\text{W m}^{-3}
$$

with densities in particles per cubic metre. It goes as density squared,
because it takes an electron and an ion meeting, and as <Katex tex="\sqrt{T}" />,
because faster electrons meet more ions. The second panel shows real places:

- **Galaxy clusters.** The space between the galaxies of a cluster is filled
  with gas at tens of millions of degrees, holding several times more mass
  than all the stars. X-ray telescopes see it as a huge, smooth glow of
  bremsstrahlung. It is so thin that, outside the densest centres, it would
  take longer than the age of the universe to cool.
- **The Sun.** The million-degree corona glows in soft X-rays, and solar
  flares heat loops of gas to tens of millions of degrees. In a flare,
  electrons fired down the loops hit the denser gas below and light up the
  loops' feet in hard X-ray bremsstrahlung.
- **Star-forming nebulae.** At 10,000 K, gas like the Orion Nebula would
  glow most in the infrared, but its bremsstrahlung is best seen in radio,
  where it shines straight through the dust around it.
- **Fusion reactors.** Bremsstrahlung is one of the main ways a fusion
  plasma loses heat. Below about 50 million degrees, a deuterium–tritium
  plasma radiates energy faster than fusion makes it, which sets how hot a
  reactor has to run.

## The same light, different pushes

Bremsstrahlung is one member of a family. An electron bent by a magnetic
field gives off [synchrotron radiation](/posts/synchrotron-radiation); one
following a curved field line, [curvature radiation](/posts/curvature-radiation);
one shaken by a light wave scatters it, [Thomson](/posts/thomson-scattering)
or [Compton scattering](/posts/compton-scattering). The rule underneath is the
same each time: accelerate a charge, and it shines.

At very high energies, bremsstrahlung turns dramatic. A cosmic-ray electron
hitting the upper atmosphere makes gamma rays, which make electron–positron
pairs, which make more gamma rays: a particle shower. Even thunderstorms
manage it: electrons accelerated in their electric fields give off flashes
of gamma rays, which the Fermi space telescope catches from orbit.
