A magnetic field can't speed an electron up or slow it down, but it can
bend its path. An electron moving across a field curves round into a
circle, or a helix if it also drifts along the field. Anything charged
that changes direction radiates, so a circling electron glows. When the
electron is slow, that glow is gentle and comes out at one frequency, the
rate it goes round. When the electron moves at nearly the speed of light,
the glow becomes synchrotron radiation: a tight beam sweeping round like a
lighthouse, with a spectrum that can stretch from radio waves to gamma
rays.

## Why a fast electron becomes a lighthouse

At everyday speeds a circling electron radiates in a broad doughnut
pattern, the same in front and behind. As it approaches the speed of
light, relativity squeezes almost all of that radiation forward, into a
cone of half-angle about <Katex tex="1/\gamma" /> around the direction it
is moving, where <Katex tex="\gamma" /> is its Lorentz factor. A 5 GeV
electron has <Katex tex="\gamma \approx 10{,}000" />, so its beam is only
about 20 arcseconds wide.

An observer off to the side only catches the beam for the brief moment it
sweeps past, once per orbit. The flash is short for two reasons: the beam
is narrow, and the electron is chasing its own light towards the observer,
which squashes the flash in time by a further factor of about
<Katex tex="\gamma^2" />. A flash about <Katex tex="1/\gamma^3" /> of an
orbit long contains frequencies about <Katex tex="\gamma^3" /> times
higher than the orbit itself. That is why a cosmic-ray electron that goes
round once every few minutes can shine at gigahertz radio frequencies.

The animation shows this: the beam sweeps past the observer on the right,
and the trace along the bottom shows what they receive. Drag the electron
energy down to <Katex tex="\gamma \approx 1" /> and the flashes melt into a
smooth wave. That slow case is called cyclotron radiation.

## The formulas

The electron's basic frequency is set by the field:

$$
\nu_g = \frac{eB}{2\pi m_e} \approx 2.8~\text{MHz per gauss}
$$

A relativistic electron goes round more slowly than that, at
<Katex tex="\nu_g/\gamma" />, but radiates up to the critical frequency

$$
\nu_c = \frac{3}{2}\,\gamma^2\,\nu_g\,\sin\alpha
$$

where <Katex tex="\alpha" /> is the pitch angle between the electron's
path and the field. One electron's spectrum rises slowly, peaks at about
<Katex tex="0.29\,\nu_c" />, and falls off sharply above
<Katex tex="\nu_c" />. The power it radiates is

$$
P = 2\,\sigma_T\,c\,\beta^2\gamma^2\,\frac{B^2}{2\mu_0}\,\sin^2\alpha
$$

so the most energetic electrons in the strongest fields lose their energy
fastest. Averaged over pitch angles the cooling time is about
<Katex tex="7.7\times 10^{8}~\text{s} / (B_{\text{G}}^2\,\gamma)" />.

## Where it shines

- **The Milky Way's radio glow.** Cosmic-ray electrons with a few GeV of
  energy, spiralling in the Galaxy's weak field of about 5 microgauss,
  radiate at around a gigahertz. Most of the radio sky away from the
  Galactic plane's hot gas is synchrotron light. Hannes Alfvén, Nicolai
  Herlofson and Karl-Otto Kiepenheuer proposed this in 1950.
- **The Crab Nebula.** Iosif Shklovsky suggested in 1953 that the
  nebula's blue-white glow was synchrotron light, and the strong
  polarization found the next year confirmed it. Its X-ray-emitting
  electrons are so energetic that they radiate away their energy in less
  than a century, which is why the Crab looks smaller in X-rays than in
  visible light: the X-rays only come from near the pulsar that keeps
  supplying fresh electrons.
- **Jets.** The jet shooting out of the galaxy M87 is synchrotron light
  from radio to X-rays, from electrons accelerated near its central black
  hole.
- **Jupiter.** Electrons trapped in Jupiter's radiation belts make it one
  of the brightest radio sources in the sky at decimetre wavelengths.

Synchrotron light also has two fingerprints that give it away. It is
strongly polarized, at right angles to the magnetic field, so radio
astronomers use it to map magnetic fields across galaxies. And because a
whole population of electrons spans a wide range of energies, the
individual spectra add up to a smooth power law, brightness falling with
frequency roughly as <Katex tex="\nu^{-0.7}" />, not the thermal hump a
hot object gives.

The same electrons can also scatter their own synchrotron photons up to
gamma-ray energies; see this site's
[Compton and inverse Compton scattering](/posts/compton-scattering) story.

## On Earth

Synchrotron radiation was first seen on 24 April 1947, as a spot of light
inside the glass vacuum tube of a 70 MeV electron synchrotron at General
Electric's lab in Schenectady, New York, which gave it its name. For
particle physicists it was a nuisance: energy lost by every electron on
every turn. Today dozens of synchrotron light sources around the world
are built to make it on purpose, bending electron beams of a few GeV in
strong magnets to produce intense X-rays for studying proteins, materials
and fossils.
