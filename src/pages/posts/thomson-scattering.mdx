Light is a wave of electric and magnetic fields. When it passes a free
electron, the electric field pushes the electron back and forth at the
light's own frequency. A shaking charge is a tiny antenna, and it sends
out light of its own in all directions. That re-radiated light is
Thomson scattering. It is the simplest way light and matter interact,
and it shaped the universe: for its first 380,000 years Thomson scattering
kept the cosmos as opaque as the inside of a star.

## What the electron does

The animation shows light arriving from the left and an electron shaking
in it. An oscillating charge radiates most strongly at right angles to its
motion and not at all along it. Choose light polarized in the plane of the
screen and the electron shakes up and down: an observer straight above or
below sees nothing, because they are looking along its line of motion.
Choose light polarized out of the screen and the electron shakes towards
and away from you, so in the plane of the screen it radiates equally in
every direction.

Ordinary unpolarized light, like sunlight, is a mix of both. Averaged
together, the scattered light is

$$
I(\theta) \propto \frac{1 + \cos^2\theta}{2}
$$

where <Katex tex="\theta" /> is the angle from the original direction. It's
equally bright straight ahead and straight back, and half as bright
sideways. And the sideways light is polarized. An observer at
<Katex tex="90^\circ" /> only sees the part of the shaking that is side-on
to them, so all the light reaching them vibrates in the same direction.
In general the degree of polarization is

$$
\Pi = \frac{\sin^2\theta}{1 + \cos^2\theta}
$$

Two things don't happen. The scattered light keeps its colour: as long as
the photon's energy is well below the electron's rest energy of 511 keV,
the electron hands it back unchanged. (Above that, the electron recoils and
it becomes [Compton scattering](/posts/compton-scattering).) And it treats
every wavelength the same, unlike the scattering by air molecules that
makes the sky blue.

## How big is an electron, to light?

The chance of a photon hitting an electron works as if the electron were a
small target of a fixed area, the Thomson cross-section:

$$
\sigma_T = \frac{8\pi}{3}\,r_e^2 = 6.65 \times 10^{-29}~\text{m}^2
$$

<Katex tex="r_e = 2.8" /> femtometres is the classical electron radius, a
length that comes out of the electron's charge and mass. Protons scatter
too, but the effect depends on how easily the charge is shaken, so a
proton, 1,836 times heavier, has a cross-section about 3.4 million times
smaller. In an ionised gas, the electrons do nearly all the scattering.
J. J. Thomson worked this out in 1906, and used the scattering of X-rays to
make one of the first estimates of how many electrons an atom holds.

## Through a cloud of electrons

One electron is a tiny target, but there are a lot of them. Through a gas
with <Katex tex="n_e" /> electrons per cubic metre, a photon travels on
average a mean free path <Katex tex="\lambda = 1/(n_e \sigma_T)" /> before
hitting one. The optical depth <Katex tex="\tau = n_e \sigma_T L" />
counts how many mean free paths there are across a distance
<Katex tex="L" />. Below 1, most light goes straight through. Far above 1,
a photon bounces off in a new direction every mean free path and staggers
out in a random walk, taking about <Katex tex="\tau^2" /> steps and a time
of about <Katex tex="\tau L / c" />.

The second panel works this out for real places:

- **Inside the Sun.** At the core's density a photon travels only a
  fraction of a millimetre between scatterings. The energy made in the
  core takes something like a hundred thousand years to random-walk out,
  instead of the two seconds a straight path would take.
- **The solar corona.** So thin that only a few millionths of the Sun's
  light is scattered towards us. That faint, polarized white glow is the
  corona we see in a total eclipse.
- **The early universe.** Until about 380,000 years after the Big Bang the
  universe was hot enough to keep atoms ionised, and free electrons
  scattered light everywhere. When it cooled enough for electrons to join
  nuclei, the fog cleared, and the light that last scattered then reaches
  us today as the cosmic microwave background. Its faint polarization
  pattern, mapped by the WMAP and Planck satellites, is Thomson scattering
  at work.
- **Intergalactic gas today.** Across the whole observable universe the
  thin gas between galaxies scatters only a fraction of a percent of
  light. Including the denser past, Planck measured the total optical
  depth since the first stars reionised the universe at about 0.05.

Thomson scattering also sets the most famous speed limit in astrophysics.
Radiation pushes on the electrons of infalling gas through this same
cross-section, and above the [Eddington luminosity](/posts/eddington-luminosity-calculator)
that push beats gravity. On Earth, fusion researchers fire lasers into
their plasmas and read the electrons' temperature and density from the
slightly Doppler-broadened Thomson-scattered light.
