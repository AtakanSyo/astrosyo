Fire a cannonball fast enough, straight up, sideways, or at any angle at
all, and there's a speed beyond which it never comes back — not because
it out-races gravity, but because it has exactly enough energy to coast
to infinity with nothing left over. That speed is the escape velocity,
and it depends on nothing but the mass you're leaving and how far from
its center you start.

## The formula

$$
v_{\rm esc} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}
$$

This falls straight out of energy conservation: set kinetic energy equal
to the magnitude of gravitational potential energy, <Katex tex="\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = GMm/r" />, and the
test mass <Katex tex="m" /> cancels out entirely. That cancellation is the whole story —
it's why escape velocity applies equally to a pebble and a spacecraft
launched from the same radius.

## Direction doesn't matter — and neither does the formula know about air

Two things about this formula surprise people the most:

- **It's an energy condition, not a trajectory.** Nothing in
  <Katex tex="\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = GMm/r" /> says which way you're pointed. Straight up, sideways, at
  45°, even briefly downward before curving away — the same speed at the
  same <Katex tex="r" /> is enough, ignoring drag and other bodies. "Escape velocity" is
  really "escape speed."
- **It says nothing about atmosphere.** Earth's escape velocity is about
  11.2 km/s, but no real rocket burns straight to 11.2 km/s and coasts
  out — a real launch fights drag and gravity losses the entire way up
  through the atmosphere, so the actual delta-v budget is considerably
  higher than the bare formula suggests. This calculator gives you the
  idealized, vacuum, point-mass number; real spaceflight engineering adds
  a substantial margin on top of it.

## The endpoint: where this formula becomes a black hole

Hold the mass <Katex tex="M" /> fixed and shrink the radius <Katex tex="r" />, and <Katex tex="v_{\rm esc}" /> climbs without
bound. Shrink <Katex tex="r" /> far enough, and <Katex tex="v_{\rm esc}" /> reaches <Katex tex="c" /> — the speed of light,
the fastest anything can go. Set <Katex tex="v_{\rm esc} = c" /> in the formula above and solve
for <Katex tex="r" />:

$$
r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

That's the Schwarzschild radius, and it isn't a coincidence that it uses
the same <Katex tex="G" />, <Katex tex="M" />, and a factor of 2 — **it's the exact same equation,
solved for the radius where escape speed hits the speed of light instead
of solved for the speed itself.** This calculator and this site's
[Schwarzschild Radius Calculator](/posts/schwarzschild-radius-calculator)
are two views of one formula. Every result on this page includes the
Schwarzschild radius for whatever mass you've entered, and how many
Schwarzschild radii away the current radius sits — the smaller that
ratio gets, the closer that mass is to being a black hole.

## Reading the comparison ladder

The ladder plots escape velocity on a log scale from a small asteroid up
through a neutron star — a span of almost **nine** orders of magnitude,
not four: Bennu's escape velocity is a gentle stroll (about 20 cm/s, a
figure NASA's OSIRIS-REx team liked to point out — you could jog fast
enough to leave it), while a typical neutron star's sits at roughly
60% of the speed of light. Between them: the Moon, Mars, Earth, Jupiter,
the Sun, and a white dwarf, each a stepping stone in the same climb from
"toy gravity" to "relativity is no longer optional."
