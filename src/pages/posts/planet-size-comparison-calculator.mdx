Pictures of the Solar System almost never show sizes to scale, because
at true scale the small worlds disappear next to the large ones. Pick
any two bodies above, from the Sun down to Pluto's moon Charon, and
the calculator draws them to the same scale and puts numbers on the
difference.

## How the numbers work

Everything comes from the two radii, <Katex tex="R_1" /> and
<Katex tex="R_2" />. The diameter ratio is the simple
"how many times wider":

$$
\frac{D_1}{D_2} = \frac{R_1}{R_2}
$$

Volume grows with the cube of the radius, which is why the
"how many fit inside" numbers get so large so quickly:

$$
\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \left(\frac{R_1}{R_2}\right)^3
$$

Jupiter is about 11 times wider than Earth, so it has about
<Katex tex="11^3 \approx 1{,}321" /> times its volume. The Sun is
about 109 times wider than Earth, so about 1.3 million Earths would
fit inside it.

## Fitting whole planets inside

The volume ratio assumes you could melt the small body down and pour it
in. Packed as solid spheres, there would be gaps between them: randomly
packed spheres fill only about 64% of a space. The calculator gives
both numbers, so about 1,321 Earths fit inside Jupiter by volume, but
only about 846 as whole planets.

## Size isn't mass

Two bodies of similar size can have very different masses. Saturn is
nearly as wide as Jupiter but has less than a third of its mass, and
its average density is lower than water's. Earth is the densest body in
the Solar System at about 5.5 g/cm³. Surface gravity depends on both:

$$
g = \frac{GM}{R^2}
$$

That's why Mercury, with a dense iron core, has almost the same surface
gravity as the larger Mars.
