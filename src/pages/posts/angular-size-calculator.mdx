The Moon and the Sun look almost exactly the same size in Earth's sky.
Not because they're actually similar in size (the Sun is about 400 times
wider), but because the Sun also happens to be about 400 times farther
away, which by the way, is 
a very interesting coincidence. 

That's the whole idea behind angular size: how big something *looks*
depends on both its true size and its distance, and you can't tell which
from the angle alone.

## The geometry

Picture a flat object of true diameter <Katex tex="D" />, centered a distance <Katex tex="d" /> away and
facing the observer broadside-on — its diameter perpendicular to the line
of sight, like a disc or a ruler held up, not a ball the observer could
stand inside of. The angle it subtends, <Katex tex="\theta" />, is the apex angle of the
triangle formed by the observer and the two ends of the object. The
triangle splits into two right triangles with the right angle at the
object's own center, adjacent side <Katex tex="d" />, and opposite side <Katex tex="D/2" />:

$$
\tan\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) = \frac{D/2}{d} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \theta = 2\arctan\!\left(\frac{D}{2d}\right)
$$

This is exact for any angle from 0° up to (but not including) 180°, for
any physical size relative to the distance — a wider, closer object just
pushes <Katex tex="\theta" /> toward 180° without ever reaching it.

## The small-angle approximation, and when it holds

For small <Katex tex="\theta" />, <Katex tex="\tan(\theta/2) \approx \theta/2" /> (also holds for <Katex tex="\tan(\theta) \approx \theta" />, but naturally the 
approximation threshold for <Katex tex="\theta" /> doubles), and the exact relation collapses to:

$$
\theta \approx \frac{D}{d}
$$

The calculator always computes the exact value, but shows the
small-angle result and the percent difference between the approximation
and true-size side by side,
so you can see directly where the approximation is "close-enough" and
where it wouldn't be.

## Angular size vs. physical size

These are easy to conflate but describe different things. **Physical
size** is an object's actual linear diameter: a number in meters or
light-years that doesn't depend on who's looking or from where.
**Angular size** is how large it *appears*, and depends equally on
physical size and distance. The same galaxy would look larger if it were
closer, without a change in its true size at all. A measured angular size, on its own, is
not a measurement of true size; you need the distance too.
