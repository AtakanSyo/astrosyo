Curvature radiation is what you get when synchrotron radiation runs out of
room. Around a pulsar the magnetic field is around a trillion times
stronger than Earth's. In a field like that, any motion of an electron
across the field is radiated away as synchrotron light in less than a
trillionth of a second. What's left is motion along the field. The
particles slide along the field lines like beads on a wire. But the field
lines of a spinning neutron star are curved, and a particle following a
curved path is still being accelerated sideways. So it still radiates,
and this time the bend in its path comes from the shape of the field line
rather than from circling round it.

## Same physics, different curve

A charge moving at nearly the speed of light radiates into a narrow cone,
about <Katex tex="1/\gamma" /> wide, pointed along its direction of motion.
For a synchrotron electron that direction keeps turning because it
circles the field. For a curvature-radiating particle it turns because the
field line itself bends. The maths is identical: replace the radius of the
electron's circle with the radius of curvature of the field line,
<Katex tex="\rho" />. The critical frequency becomes

$$
\nu_c = \frac{3}{4\pi}\,\gamma^3\,\frac{c}{\rho}
$$

and the spectrum has exactly the synchrotron shape, peaking at about
<Katex tex="0.29\,\nu_c" />. The power each particle radiates is

$$
P = \frac{e^2 c\,\gamma^4}{6\pi\varepsilon_0\,\rho^2}
$$

The animation shows the geometry. Particles leave the neutron star's
surface along an open field line from near the magnetic pole. At every
point they shed photons along the line's current direction, so as the
line curves, the photons fan out into a broad beam. The dashed circle is
the circle that best matches the field line at that point; its radius is
<Katex tex="\rho" />. As the star spins, that beam sweeps across the sky,
and we see the star pulse.

## Pulsar gamma rays

Pulsars have huge electric fields along their field lines that push
particles to enormous energies, but the <Katex tex="\gamma^4" /> in the
power formula fights back. The faster a particle goes, the harder it
radiates, until the energy it gains from the field each second equals what
it loses. For pulsar conditions that happens near
<Katex tex="\gamma \approx 10^7" />, particles carrying about 5 TeV each. On field
lines curved over about a thousand kilometres, that puts the peak in gamma
rays of hundreds of MeV to a few GeV. The Fermi gamma-ray space telescope
has found hundreds of pulsars shining at exactly those energies, with
spectra that cut off sharply at a few GeV, just as this picture predicts.
Whether the gamma rays come from curvature radiation or from synchrotron
radiation further out, near where the field lines open, is still being
argued over; both leave a similar fingerprint.

Many of those gamma rays don't escape. A gamma ray crossing a strong
magnetic field can turn into an electron–positron pair, and those new
particles slide along field lines and radiate gamma rays of their own. The
cascade fills the pulsar's surroundings with pairs, the plasma that powers
everything else the pulsar does.

## Pulsar radio and fast radio bursts

The same process at much lower energy, with <Katex tex="\gamma" /> of a few
hundred, lands in the radio. But pulsar radio pulses are so intense that
no collection of particles radiating independently could produce them.
The leading explanation is coherent curvature radiation: bunches of
particles sliding along a field line together, radiating in step like a
single giant charge. A bunch of <Katex tex="N" /> particles radiating in
step gives <Katex tex="N^2" /> times the power of one, not
<Katex tex="N" /> times. The same mechanism, near magnetars with even
stronger fields, is one of the main proposed explanations for fast radio
bursts. In April 2020 a magnetar in our own galaxy, SGR 1935+2154, gave
off a burst of this kind, the first ever seen from a known source, and
coherent radiation from its magnetic field is one of the candidates still
under debate.
