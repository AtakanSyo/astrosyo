You can't see the compact object in an X-ray binary directly — no
telescope resolves a black hole or neutron star that way. What you
*can* see is its visible companion star wobbling back and forth,
Doppler-shifted by the pull of something orbiting it. From just that
wobble's period and speed, one number — the binary mass function —
tells you something concrete about the invisible thing on the other
end, without needing to know the system's orientation in space at all.

The mass function below is a generalization of a more basic relation:
[Kepler's Third Law Calculator](/posts/kepler-third-law-calculator)
solves the plain two-body version, <Katex tex="P^2 = \frac{4\pi^2 a^3}{G(M_1+M_2)}" />, for period,
semi-major axis, or total mass directly — worth a look for the simpler
case where you don't need the inclination-dependent machinery here.

## The mass function

$$
f(M) = \frac{PK^3}{2\pi G}(1-e^2)^{3/2} = \frac{M_2^3 \sin^3 i}{(M_1+M_2)^2}
$$

<Katex tex="P" /> is the orbital period, <Katex tex="K" /> the visible star's radial-velocity
semi-amplitude (how fast its line-of-sight speed swings from the orbit),
and <Katex tex="e" /> the orbital eccentricity. The right-hand side is where the physics
lives: <Katex tex="M_1" /> and <Katex tex="M_2" /> are the two stars' masses, and <Katex tex="i" /> is the orbital
inclination — how tilted the orbit's plane is relative to our line of
sight. Radial velocity only measures the *line-of-sight* component of
orbital motion, which is why <Katex tex="\sin i" /> (and, cubed, <Katex tex="\sin^3 i" />) appears: at
<Katex tex="i = 90^\circ" /> (edge-on) we see the full orbital speed; at <Katex tex="i = 0^\circ" /> (face-on) the
star moves entirely across the sky and <Katex tex="K" /> would be zero regardless of
how fast it's actually orbiting.

## The result that needs no assumptions at all

Both <Katex tex="M_1" /> and <Katex tex="i" /> are usually unknown. But look at what <Katex tex="f(M)" /> can never
exceed: because <Katex tex="M_1 \ge 0" /> makes <Katex tex="(M_1+M_2)^2 \ge M_2^2" />, and <Katex tex="\sin i \le 1" />,

$$
f(M) = \frac{M_2^3 \sin^3 i}{(M_1+M_2)^2} \le \frac{M_2^3}{M_2^2} = M_2
$$

**<Katex tex="f(M)" /> is always a lower bound on <Katex tex="M_2" />** — true for *any* <Katex tex="M_1" /> and *any*
inclination, no assumptions required. This is exactly the argument
historically used to build the case for Cygnus X-1: even the bare mass
function, computed from nothing but the optical star's period and
velocity, already exceeded what a neutron star could plausibly weigh.

## Solving for an exact companion mass

If you're willing to estimate the visible star's mass (usually from its
spectral type) and assume an inclination (from eclipse timing, ellipsoidal
light-curve variations, or a model fit), the relation becomes a genuine
cubic equation in <Katex tex="M_2" />, which this calculator solves numerically rather
than approximating. Try it on the Cygnus X-1 preset: with the donor
star's mass (~40.6 M☉) and the system's measured inclination (~27.5°)
plugged in, the mass function alone (<Katex tex="f(M) \approx 0.25" /> M☉ from <Katex tex="P \approx 5.6" /> days and
<Katex tex="K \approx 75.6" /> km/s) resolves to a companion mass of about 21 M☉ — matching
modern measurements of the black hole at Cygnus X-1's heart.

## Reading the two charts

- **The mass ruler** shades the entire range <Katex tex="M_2" /> is allowed to occupy —
  everything at or above <Katex tex="f(M)" /> — next to a few reference masses (a
  typical neutron star, the rough maximum a neutron star's equation of
  state can support, and a typical stellar-mass black hole). Seeing the
  shaded region swallow the neutron-star landmarks entirely is a much
  more direct argument than the number alone.
- **The inclination-dependence curve** shows exactly how sensitive the
  solved <Katex tex="M_2" /> is to the one thing you usually can't measure directly: at
  low inclination (near face-on), the required companion mass blows up,
  since a small <Katex tex="\sin^3 i" /> has to be compensated by a much larger <Katex tex="M_2" /> to match
  the same observed <Katex tex="f(M)" />. Where the curve sits above the ~3 M☉ line,
  no plausible inclination lets the companion be a neutron star.
