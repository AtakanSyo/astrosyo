When you observe a galaxy at redshift <Katex tex="z = 1" />, you're not seeing it as it
is now — you're seeing light that left it long before Earth existed.
"How long ago" is the lookback time, and unlike most of the conversions
on this site, it isn't a formula you can rearrange with algebra. It's an
integral over the universe's entire expansion history between then and
now, and the answer depends on which cosmology you plug in.

## The integral

In a flat universe with matter and a cosmological constant, the
expansion rate at redshift <Katex tex="z" /> relative to today is

$$
E(z) = \sqrt{\Omega_m (1+z)^3 + \Omega_\Lambda}
$$

and the lookback time to that redshift is

$$
t_L(z) = \frac{1}{H_0} \int_0^z \frac{dz'}{(1+z')E(z')}
$$

There's no closed-form antiderivative for this in general — it has to
be evaluated numerically. This calculator does that with Simpson's rule
at a resolution far finer than the answer's precision requires, so the
numerical method itself isn't the source of any meaningful error; what
you get back is limited by how well your chosen <Katex tex="\Omega_m" />, <Katex tex="\Omega_\Lambda" />, and <Katex tex="H_0" /> actually
describe the real universe, not by the integration.

## Why the cosmology matters

Change <Katex tex="H_0" />, <Katex tex="\Omega_m" />, or <Katex tex="\Omega_\Lambda" />, and every lookback time changes with them — this
is the calculator's central point, not an edge case. The clearest way to
see it: pick <Katex tex="z = 1" /> under Planck 2018 parameters (<Katex tex="H_0 = 67.4" />, <Katex tex="\Omega_m = 0.315" />)
and you get a lookback time of about 7.95 billion years, meaning the
object's light left it about 5.85 billion years after the Big Bang. Keep
<Katex tex="z = 1" /> but switch to an Einstein–de Sitter universe (<Katex tex="\Omega_m = 1" />, no dark
energy at all — the standard cosmology before the late-1990s supernova
observations) and the lookback time drops to about 6.0 billion years,
because a matter-only universe has a very different expansion history.
Same redshift, different physical answer — which is exactly why every
result here is reported next to the parameters that produced it, and why
serious work always states its assumed cosmology explicitly.

## Reading the two charts

- **The cosmic timeline** places the emission time directly on a bar
  running from the Big Bang to today, next to a few landmark epochs
  (the CMB, the first galaxies, when the Solar System formed) — turning
  "5.85 Gyr after the Big Bang" into a point on a story whose overall
  shape you likely already know.
- **The integrand chart** plots <Katex tex="1/[(1+z')E(z')]" /> itself and shades the
  region from 0 to <Katex tex="z" />. That shaded area, multiplied by the Hubble time
  <Katex tex="1/H_0" />, *is* the lookback time — a direct, visual answer to "why isn't
  this just algebra."

## Validated against Astropy

Because this result is entirely dependent on the numerical integration
and the assumed cosmological model, its outputs were checked directly
against [Astropy's](https://www.astropy.org/) `cosmology` module before
publication — specifically `FlatLambdaCDM` with radiation switched off
(`Tcmb0=0`), matching this calculator's matter+<Katex tex="\Lambda" />-only model exactly.
Across redshifts from 0.1 to 10 and four different cosmologies (Planck
2018, WMAP9, a simple <Katex tex="H_0=70" />/<Katex tex="\Omega_m=0.3" /> model, and Einstein–de Sitter), both
the lookback time and the age of the universe agreed with Astropy's
values to better than 1 part in 10⁶ — comfortably beyond the precision
either tool displays. Radiation is omitted from both, so treat results
at very high redshift (<Katex tex="z \gtrsim 1000" />, near recombination) as approximate; at
the redshifts most real observational targets sit at, that omission is
negligible.
