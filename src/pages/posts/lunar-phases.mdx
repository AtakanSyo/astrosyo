<p>The Moon has been our closest celestial companion for billions of years. Though, maybe some of you have noticed, 
  it changes appearance; from 
  thin crescents to bright full moons. This cycle has inspired calendars, festivals, and myths across human history.</p>

<h2>Why Does the Moon Have Phases?</h2>

<p>The Moon doesn’t produce light; it reflects sunlight. As the Moon orbits Earth, we see different portions of its sunlit 
  half, depending on the geometry between the Sun, the Earth (our viewpoint), and the Moon itself. Even though the 
  Moon always shows the same face toward Earth due to tidal locking, the illuminated fraction changes continuously 
  over roughly 29.53 days.</p>

<p>In essence, the phase we see is determined by the angle between the Sun and the Earth as viewed from the Moon. 
  This shifting 
  geometry creates the gradual transition we observe from thin crescents to full illumination.</p>

<MoonPhaseDiagram client:visible />

<p>The diagram looks down on the Earth and the Moon from above the north pole. Seen from up there, the Moon is always
  half lit: the side facing the Sun. What changes is how much of that lit half is turned toward the Earth. The small
  box in the corner shows the Moon as it looks from the Earth at that moment, and the triangle marks the angle
  between the Sun and the Moon in our sky. When the angle is zero we get a New Moon, at a right angle a Quarter Moon,
  and when the Moon is opposite the Sun, a Full Moon.</p>

<p>The diagram is not to scale. The real Sun is about 400 times farther away than the Moon, so its light reaches both
  as parallel rays. It also looks as if the Earth should block the sunlight at Full Moon. It usually does not, because
  the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5 degrees and the Moon passes above or below the Earth's shadow. When it does
  pass through the shadow, we see a lunar eclipse.</p>

<h2>The Eight Phases</h2>

<p>During a full lunar cycle, the Moon transitions through eight commonly recognized stages. These include the New Moon, 
  where the Moon lies between Earth and the Sun and appears completely dark; the Waxing Crescent, when a thin arc of 
  sunlight begins to appear on the right; the First Quarter, in which half of the lunar face becomes illuminated; 
  and the Waxing Gibbous phase, where the bright region grows larger.</p>

<p>At the midpoint lies the Full Moon, when the Earth is positioned between the Sun and the Moon and the entire near 
  side reflects sunlight. After this, the cycle reverses: the Waning Gibbous phase brings 
  a gradual shrink in brightness, followed by the Last Quarter, where the left half is illuminated, 
  and finally the Waning Crescent, the final thin arc before returning to the New Moon.</p>
