How bright a star looks from Earth (its apparent magnitude, <Katex tex="m" />) depends
on two completely different things tangled together: how bright it
actually is, and how far away it is. Absolute magnitude (<Katex tex="M" />) untangles
them by asking a hypothetical question — how bright would this object
look if it were exactly 10 parsecs away? The gap between the two
answers, <Katex tex="m - M" />, turns out to depend on nothing but distance (and dust),
which makes it one of the most directly useful relations in
observational astronomy: measure any two of <Katex tex="m" />, <Katex tex="M" />, and <Katex tex="d" />, and the third
falls out immediately.

## The relation

$$
m - M = 5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\ \mathrm{pc}}\right)
$$

<Katex tex="m - M" /> is called the **distance modulus**, often written <Katex tex="\mu" />. It's zero
exactly at 10 pc (where <Katex tex="m" /> and <Katex tex="M" /> agree by definition), negative for
anything closer, and grows by 5 for every factor of 10 farther away —
so a star with <Katex tex="m = 10" /> and <Katex tex="M = 5" /> has <Katex tex="\mu = 5" />, meaning it sits at exactly
100 pc, the worked example this calculator opens with.

This is also precisely how the "standard candle" method for measuring
cosmic distances works: if you know an object's absolute magnitude
independently (Cepheid variables and Type Ia supernovae are the classic
examples, calibrated through the cosmic distance ladder), a single
apparent-magnitude measurement is enough to read off its distance.

## Adding interstellar extinction

Dust and gas between here and the object absorb and scatter its light,
making it look fainter than distance alone would predict — never
brighter. Accounting for it adds one term:

$$
m - M = 5 \log_{10}\left(\frac{d}{10\ \mathrm{pc}}\right) + A
$$

<Katex tex="A" /> is the **extinction**, in magnitudes, along that specific line of
sight. Skip it (<Katex tex="A = 0" />) and the relation is exact for an unobstructed
view; ignore it when it's actually present and a distance calculated
from <Katex tex="m" /> and <Katex tex="M" /> alone comes out too large, because some of the "distance
dimming" you measured was really dust dimming. This calculator makes <Katex tex="A" />
an explicit optional toggle rather than folding it in silently, so it's
always clear whether a given result assumes a clear line of sight or
not.

## Reading the two charts

- **The distance ladder** places the computed distance on a single log
  scale next to a handful of well-known real distances — the nearest
  star, the Pleiades, the Galactic center, the Large Magellanic Cloud,
  Andromeda, the Virgo Cluster — anchored on the 10 pc point where
  absolute magnitude is defined. Seeing "163 pc" land visibly past the
  Pleiades and well short of the Galactic center does more for intuition
  than the bare number does alone.
- **The distance-modulus chart** plots <Katex tex="\mu" /> against distance on a semi-log
  axis, where the fixed +5-per-decade slope in the formula becomes an
  literal straight line. With extinction switched on, a second dashed
  line shows where the same object would sit with a perfectly clear line
  of sight — the vertical gap between the two points is exactly <Katex tex="A" />,
  turning "how much extinction is affecting this measurement" into a
  distance on the page rather than an abstract correction term.
