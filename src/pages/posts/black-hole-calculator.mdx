A black hole is described by remarkably few numbers. Its mass and its
spin (and in principle its electric charge, which for real black holes
is essentially zero) are enough to work out everything else. This
calculator brings together everything the site's individual black hole
calculators compute, from one mass and one spin. Each linked row opens
the dedicated page with the full derivation.

## Size

$$
r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

The Schwarzschild radius is the event horizon of a black hole that
isn't spinning: about 3 km per solar mass. Sagittarius A*, at the
centre of the Milky Way, has 4.3 million solar masses and a radius of
about 12.7 million km, around 18 times the Sun's radius. A spinning
black hole's horizon is smaller, down to half of <Katex tex="r_s" /> at
the maximum spin.

Two more radii matter for anything near it:

- **The photon sphere**, at <Katex tex="1.5\,r_s" />, where light can
  orbit. It's what the Event Horizon Telescope images outline.
- **The innermost stable circular orbit (ISCO)**, at
  <Katex tex="3\,r_s" /> without spin. Inside it, gas can't orbit and
  falls in, so it marks the inner edge of an accretion disk. Spin moves
  it inward for gas orbiting the same way.

## Bigger is less dense

The radius grows in proportion to the mass, but volume grows with its
cube, so the average density inside the horizon falls as
<Katex tex="1/M^2" />. A stellar-mass black hole is far denser than an
atomic nucleus. Around 140 million solar masses the average comes down
to about the density of water, and the largest black holes known are
thinner than air.

## Falling in

What kills you near a black hole is the tidal stretch: the pull on your
feet being stronger than on your head. Across a person of length
<Katex tex="L" /> at distance <Katex tex="r" />:

$$
\Delta a \approx \frac{2GML}{r^3}
$$

Near a small black hole that stretch reaches lethal levels thousands of
kilometres before the horizon, which is the "spaghettification" of
popular science. Near a supermassive one the horizon is so far out that
the stretch there is less than you feel standing up; you would cross it
without noticing. The calculator uses about 10 g of head-to-foot
difference as the lethal threshold, a rough figure.

The same reasoning applies to stars. Below roughly 100 million solar
masses, a black hole tears a Sun-like star apart outside its horizon
and the debris lights up as a tidal disruption flare; above it, the
star is swallowed whole. The
[Tidal Disruption Radius Calculator](/posts/tidal-disruption-radius-calculator)
covers other kinds of star.

## Hawking radiation

Black holes slowly radiate, with a temperature inversely proportional
to their mass:

$$
T = \frac{\hbar c^3}{8\pi G M k_B}
$$

A black hole of one solar mass sits at 60 billionths of a kelvin, far
colder than the 2.7 K cosmic microwave background, so for now it
absorbs more than it emits. Only very small black holes, below about
the Moon's mass, are hotter than the background and actually shrink.
Their evaporation time grows as <Katex tex="M^3" />: a solar-mass black
hole would take about <Katex tex="10^{67}" /> years.
