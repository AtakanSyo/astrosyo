Anything that spins — a planet, a hard drive platter, a neutron star —
shares a single number describing how fast it turns, regardless of its
size: angular velocity. Two objects can have wildly different physical
sizes and still turn at exactly the same rate, and this calculator
converts between every common way of expressing that rate.

## The relation

$$
\omega = \frac{\Delta\theta}{\Delta t}
$$

For steady rotation, one full turn is 2π radians, which gives the
everyday form:

$$
\omega = \frac{2\pi}{T} = 2\pi f
$$

<Katex tex="T" /> is the rotation period (time for one full turn), <Katex tex="f" /> the rotational
frequency (turns per unit time). Earth turns once every 23h 56m 4s (a
*sidereal* day — slightly shorter than the familiar 24-hour solar day,
because Earth also has to catch up with its own orbital motion around
the Sun to bring the Sun back to the same spot in the sky) — that's
<Katex tex="\omega \approx 7.29 \times 10^{-5}" /> rad/s. A 7200 RPM hard drive spins at <Katex tex="\omega \approx 754" /> rad/s —
roughly ten million times faster.

## Same ω, very different speeds

Every point on a rigid rotating body shares that one <Katex tex="\omega" />. What doesn't
stay the same is tangential (straight-line) speed:

$$
v = \omega r
$$

A point twice as far from the axis moves twice as fast, in an ordinary
meters-per-second sense, even though it completes exactly the same
number of rotations per second as every closer point. This is why
Earth's equator (radius ≈6,378 km) is actually moving at about
1,674 km/h due to rotation alone, while a point near either pole is
essentially standing still despite sharing the exact same <Katex tex="\omega" />.

## When rotation gets extreme

Neutron stars make this concrete in a startling way. The Crab Pulsar
spins about 30 times per second — already fast enough that a point on
its ~10 km surface moves at roughly 0.6% of the speed of light. The
fastest confirmed pulsar, PSR J1748-2446ad, spins **716 times per
second** — and a point on its surface would be moving at roughly
**15% of the speed of light**, purely from rotation. These are real
measured values, not exaggerations; a neutron star's small size is
exactly what makes such extreme spin rates survivable at all — for a
star-sized object, the same angular velocity would tear it apart, but
for a body only tens of kilometers across, the actual (tangential)
speeds involved stay merely relativistic rather than impossible.
