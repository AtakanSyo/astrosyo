Astronomy runs on a different set of rulers depending on what's being
measured. A planetary orbit is naturally described in astronomical units;
a nearby star, in light-years; the structure of the Galaxy, in kiloparsecs;
the scale of the observable universe, in gigaparsecs. Converting between
them by hand means chaining several huge or tiny multipliers together —
this tool does it in one step: enter a distance in any unit and see it
expressed in all of them at once.

## The units, defined exactly

Every conversion here comes from three fixed physical definitions, not
measured or approximate values:

$$
1\ \text{AU} = 149{,}597{,}870{,}700\ \text{m} \qquad \text{(IAU 2012, exact)}
$$

$$
1\ \text{pc} = \frac{648000}{\pi}\ \text{AU} \qquad \text{(IAU 2015 — the distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arcsecond)}
$$

$$
1\ \text{ly} = c \times 365.25\ \text{days} = 9{,}460{,}730{,}472{,}580{,}800\ \text{m}
$$

using the SI-exact speed of light, <Katex tex="c = 299{,}792{,}458" /> m/s, and the Julian
year (365.25 days of 86,400 SI seconds) that "light-year" is
conventionally defined against. Kiloparsecs, megaparsecs, and gigaparsecs
are simply 10³, 10⁶, and 10⁹ parsecs. Because all of these are
*definitions* rather than measurements, every conversion below is exact to
floating-point precision — nothing here depends on an external API or a
value that could later be refined.

## Light-travel time

Distance and travel time are two sides of the same number once you fix a
speed: light covers one light-year in exactly one year, by definition, so
dividing any distance by <Katex tex="c" /> gives an intuitive second read on its scale —
seconds for the Earth-Moon distance, minutes for the Earth-Sun distance,
years for the nearest stars, and billions of years for the edge of the
observable universe.
