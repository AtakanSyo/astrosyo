Every telescope is described by two lengths: its aperture, the
diameter of the main lens or mirror, and its focal length, the
distance over which that lens or mirror brings light to a focus. The
ratio between them, the focal ratio, decides a lot about how the
telescope behaves at the eyepiece.

## The formula

$$
N = \frac{F}{D}
$$

<Katex tex="F" /> is the focal length and <Katex tex="D" /> the
aperture, in the same unit. An 8" Dobsonian with a 203 mm mirror and
1200 mm focal length works out to <Katex tex="1200 / 203 \approx 5.9" />,
written f/5.9. Rearranged, the same formula gives the focal length
(<Katex tex="F = N \times D" />) when you know the f-number and
aperture, or the aperture (<Katex tex="D = F / N" />) when you know
the other two. Most telescopes print both numbers on the tube or in
the spec sheet, often as "203/1200" or "D = 203 mm, F = 1200 mm".

## Fast, medium and slow

The terms come from photography, where a low f-number means a shorter
exposure. For visual observing the rough bands are:

- **Fast (below f/5):** short tube for the aperture, wide fields of
  view, good for large deep-sky objects and astrophotography. Needs
  careful collimation, and Newtonians show coma at the edge of the
  field.
- **Medium (f/5 to f/8):** where most beginner Dobsonians and
  refractors sit. Forgiving with budget eyepieces and collimation.
- **Slow (above f/8):** Maksutovs, Schmidt-Cassegrains and long
  refractors. High magnification with comfortable eyepieces and sharp
  images across the field, at the cost of a narrower view.

The f-number doesn't change how bright a star looks or how faint you
can go; that depends on aperture alone. It changes the scale of the
image, which is why the same eyepiece magnifies more in a slow scope.

## Barlows and focal reducers

A Barlow lens multiplies the effective focal length (and so the focal
ratio) by its factor: a 2× Barlow turns an f/5 scope into an f/10 one
for that eyepiece. A focal reducer does the opposite. The common 0.63×
reducer for Schmidt-Cassegrains turns a C8 from 2032 mm at f/10 into
about 1280 mm at f/6.3, which widens the field and shortens
photographic exposures. Pick one from the dropdown to see the
effective numbers.
