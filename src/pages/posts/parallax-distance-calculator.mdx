Every other distance-measuring method in astronomy — standard candles,
redshift, the whole cosmic distance ladder — ultimately calibrates itself
against one technique that needs no assumptions about what a star is or
how bright it "should" be: parallax. Measure how much a star's apparent
position wiggles over a year, relative to the much farther background
stars, and you have its distance directly from geometry. It's also where
the parsec comes from — the unit is *defined* by this relation.

## The relation

$$
d(\text{pc}) = \frac{1}{p(^{\prime\prime})}
$$

A parsec is, by definition, the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle
of exactly one arcsecond. That's not a coincidence dressed up as a
formula — it's why the relation is this clean. A parallax of 100
milliarcseconds is a distance of exactly 10 pc, no approximation involved.

## The geometry

The diagram above shows why this works: as Earth orbits the Sun, a nearby
star's line of sight sweeps through a small angle relative to the much
more distant background stars, which don't move appreciably no matter
where Earth is. Compare the star's apparent position from opposite points
in Earth's orbit (classically, observations six months apart) and it
appears to have shifted — not because the star moved, but because you
did. The parallax angle <Katex tex="p" /> is defined as the shift relative to the *mean*
position (i.e., as seen from the Sun), so the full back-and-forth swing
between the two extreme epochs is <Katex tex="2p" />.

## When <Katex tex="d = 1/p" /> stops being trustworthy

Parallax measurement always carries some uncertainty, and it's tempting
to propagate it the simple way: since <Katex tex="d = 1/p" />, a fractional uncertainty
on <Katex tex="p" /> becomes the same fractional uncertainty on <Katex tex="d" />. That's exactly right
as a first-order approximation — but it's *only* a first-order
approximation, because <Katex tex="1/p" /> is a nonlinear transform. For a
roughly-Gaussian parallax measurement, the induced distribution on
distance becomes increasingly skewed as the fractional uncertainty grows,
and the naive point estimate becomes biased. Push it far enough — a
parallax consistent with zero within its error bar — and <Katex tex="1/p" /> isn't just
imprecise, it's undefined or nonsensical (a negative or infinite
"distance").

This isn't a hypothetical edge case. It's a real, well-documented issue
in modern astrometry: Gaia measures parallaxes for well over a billion
stars, and a large fraction of the faintest, most distant ones have
fractional uncertainties large enough that naive inversion measurably
biases the results. The standard fix, following Bailer-Jones (2015) and
the body of work it spawned, is Bayesian: combine the parallax
measurement with a prior on how stars are actually distributed in space,
rather than trusting <Katex tex="1/p" /> in isolation. As a rule of thumb, this
calculator treats fractional uncertainties under 10% as reliable, 10–20%
as worth caution, and anything larger as a case where simple inversion
shouldn't be trusted at face value.
