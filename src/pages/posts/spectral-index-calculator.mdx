Radio sources don't shine at one frequency — they have a whole spectrum,
and how their brightness changes across it carries real physics. A single
number, the spectral index <Katex tex="\alpha" />, captures the shape of that spectrum, and
whether it's steep, flat, or rising tells you something about the
underlying emission mechanism before you've done anything more
sophisticated than compare two flux measurements.

## The definition

A source's flux density <Katex tex="S_\nu" /> as a function of frequency <Katex tex="\nu" /> is modeled as a
power law:

$$
S_\nu \propto \nu^{\alpha}
$$

Given two measurements, <Katex tex="S_1" /> at <Katex tex="\nu_1" /> and <Katex tex="S_2" /> at <Katex tex="\nu_2" />, this rearranges to

$$
\alpha = \frac{\ln(S_2 / S_1)}{\ln(\nu_2 / \nu_1)}
$$

That same <Katex tex="\alpha" /> then predicts the flux density at any third frequency <Katex tex="\nu_3" /> via
<Katex tex="S_3 = S_1\cdot(\nu_3/\nu_1)^{\alpha}" /> — useful for planning an observation at a frequency you
haven't measured yet, or checking whether a source's known spectral shape
is consistent with a new detection.

**A note on sign convention.** This calculator uses <Katex tex="S_\nu \propto \nu^{\alpha}" />, standard in
radio survey catalogs (NVSS, VLSS, and most others). Some literature
instead defines <Katex tex="\alpha" /> through <Katex tex="S_\nu \propto \nu^{-\alpha}" /> — the opposite sign — so a quoted
"<Katex tex="\alpha = 0.8" />" can mean a rising *or* a falling spectrum depending on the
source. Always check convention before comparing values across papers.

## What the shape of the spectrum tells you

- **<Katex tex="\alpha" /> ≈ 0 (flat spectrum):** common in compact, optically-thick AGN
  cores — a superposition of many self-absorbed synchrotron components
  peaking at different frequencies can conspire to look nearly flat
  overall.
- **<Katex tex="\alpha" /> ≈ -0.5 to -1.2 (steep spectrum):** the classic signature of
  optically-thin synchrotron emission from extended structures like radio
  lobes and jets — the regime most extragalactic radio sources fall into.
- **<Katex tex="\alpha" /> ≲ -1.2 (ultra-steep spectrum):** used deliberately as a search
  criterion for high-redshift radio galaxies, since spectral steepening
  correlates statistically with distance.
- **<Katex tex="\alpha" /> > 0 (inverted spectrum):** the source is optically thick at the
  observed frequencies — young, compact, or self-absorbed sources whose
  emission hasn't yet freed itself from its own opacity.

These bands are a useful rule of thumb, not a hard boundary — real
spectra can curve, and the same source can sit in different categories at
different frequency ranges.

## Uncertainty and extrapolation

Frequencies are normally known far more precisely than flux densities, so
this calculator treats <Katex tex="\nu_1" />, <Katex tex="\nu_2" />, and <Katex tex="\nu_3" /> as exact and propagates only the
flux density uncertainties you supply. The resulting uncertainty on <Katex tex="\alpha" />
scales inversely with how far apart <Katex tex="\nu_1" /> and <Katex tex="\nu_2" /> are in log-frequency —
measurements close together in frequency give a much noisier <Katex tex="\alpha" /> than ones
spread widely apart, for the same flux precision. Extrapolating to <Katex tex="\nu_3" />
compounds <Katex tex="\alpha" />'s uncertainty further, growing with how far <Katex tex="\nu_3" /> sits from your
anchor point — a genuine feature of projecting a power law outward, not
an artifact of the calculation.
