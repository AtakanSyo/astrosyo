Compress any amount of mass into a small enough volume, and it becomes a
black hole — the exact radius where that happens is the Schwarzschild
radius. It's one of the simplest formulas in general relativity to
write down, and one of the most surprising to actually compute: Earth's
Schwarzschild radius is about the size of a marble.

## The formula

$$
r_s = \frac{2GM}{c^2}
$$

That's it — no exponent, no approximation. Double the mass and the
radius exactly doubles; this is a straight proportionality, not merely
a power law that happens to look linear. A 1 M☉ object has
<Katex tex="r_s" /> ≈ 2.95 km, so a 10 M☉ black hole's event horizon sits at almost
exactly 29.5 km — ten times the mass, ten times the radius, precisely.

## What it actually means

Every object technically has a Schwarzschild radius — it's just usually
far smaller than the object itself, meaning nothing physical happens at
that radius. The Sun's Schwarzschild radius is about 3 km, deep inside
its actual ~696,000 km surface; nothing would need to change for the Sun
to "become" a black hole unless it were somehow compressed down to that
size, which stellar physics doesn't permit for a star of its mass. Only
when a real, physical event horizon actually forms — in a collapsed
massive star's core, or at a galaxy's center — does <Katex tex="r_s" /> become the size
of something real.

## Real black holes, real scales

- **A 10 M☉ stellar-mass black hole:** <Katex tex="r_s" /> ≈ 29.5 km — smaller than most
  cities.
- **Sagittarius A\*** (the Milky Way's central black hole, ~4.3 million
  M☉): <Katex tex="r_s" /> ≈ 12.7 million km, about a fifth of Mercury's orbital
  distance from the Sun.
- **M87\*** (the first black hole ever directly imaged, ~6.5 billion
  M☉): <Katex tex="r_s" /> ≈ 19.2 billion km, or about 128 AU — larger than Pluto's
  orbit around the Sun.

## An important caveat: this is Schwarzschild, not Kerr

This formula describes an idealized **non-rotating, uncharged**
black hole. Every black hole that actually forms from stellar collapse
carries at least some spin, and a spinning (Kerr) black hole's event
horizon follows a different formula entirely:
<Katex tex="r_+ = r_g(1 + \sqrt{1-a_*^2})" />, where <Katex tex="r_g = GM/c^2 = r_s/2" /> and <Katex tex="a_*" /> is the
dimensionless spin. At zero spin this reduces to exactly <Katex tex="r_s" /> above; at
maximal spin, the horizon shrinks to just half of <Katex tex="r_s" />. For the spin-
dependent version — and to see how it relates to the innermost stable
orbit around a rotating black hole — see this site's
[Black Hole ISCO Calculator](/posts/black-hole-isco-calculator).

## The same equation, solved the other way

Escape velocity is <Katex tex="v_{\rm esc} = \sqrt{2GM/r}" /> — the speed needed to leave a
mass's gravity for good. Set <Katex tex="v_{\rm esc} = c" /> and solve for <Katex tex="r" /> instead of for
the speed, and out falls <Katex tex="r_s = 2GM/c^2" /> exactly. The Schwarzschild radius
isn't a separate fact about black holes bolted onto escape velocity —
it's the radius at which escape velocity *reaches* <Katex tex="c" />. This site's
[Escape Velocity Calculator](/posts/escape-velocity-calculator) is the
same formula approached from the other direction, complete with a
comparison ladder from a small asteroid up through a neutron star.
