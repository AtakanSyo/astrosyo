The Moon is about 384,400 km away and looks about half a degree wide. What
would we see if each planet sat at that same distance instead? The
visualization above shows an evening sky with the Moon swapped for each
planet. The view uses a real field of view, so each one appears exactly as
big as it would to your eye. The small dashed circle shows the real Moon's
size for comparison.

## How big would each planet look?

| Body | Width in the sky | Times wider than the Moon | Sky area, in Moons |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Moon | 0.5° | 1 | 1 |
| Mercury | 0.7° | 1.4 | 2 |
| Mars | 1.0° | 2 | 4 |
| Venus | 1.8° | 3.5 | 12 |
| Neptune | 7.3° | 14 | 200 |
| Uranus | 7.6° | 15 | 210 |
| Saturn (planet) | 17° | 33 | 1,100 |
| Saturn (rings) | 39° | 76 | |
| Jupiter | 21° | 40 | 1,600 |

For a sense of these angles, hold your hand out at arm's length. Your
little finger covers about 1°, and your closed fist about 10°. The Moon is
half a finger. Jupiter would be about two fists.

## Jupiter in place of the Moon

Jupiter would be about **21° across, 40 times wider than the Moon**,
covering as much sky as about 1,600 full Moons. Its cloud bands, the Great
Red Spot and the white storm ovals would all be clear to the naked eye. It
would also be brilliant. Jupiter's clouds reflect about four times as much
light as the Moon's dark rock, and it would cover so much more sky that a
"full Jupiter" would light the night roughly **7,000 times more** than a
full Moon.

## Saturn in place of the Moon

Saturn itself would be about 17° across, but its rings would steal the
show. The main rings span about 273,000 km, so at the Moon's distance they
would stretch about **39° across the sky**, more than twice the planet's
width. Over the year we would see them open and close as the angle
between us changed.

## The ice giants and the small planets

**Uranus** and **Neptune** would be blue-green discs about 7.5° across,
around 14 times wider than the Moon. **Venus**, nearly Earth's size, would
be 3.5 times wider than the Moon and dazzling white from its unbroken
clouds. **Mars** would be twice the Moon's width and clearly red.
**Mercury** is only slightly larger than the Moon, and with its similar grey,
cratered face it might take a while to notice the swap.

## Could this really happen?

Not without dramatic consequences. At the same distance, a body's tidal
pull is proportional to its mass, and the planets are much heavier than the
Moon:

- **Mars** would raise tides about 9 times stronger, and **Venus** about
  66 times.
- **Neptune** and **Uranus** would pull over 1,000 times harder.
- **Jupiter** would pull **about 26,000 times harder**. Earth's oceans
  would surge across continents, and the flexing would heat the planet's
  interior, the same process that makes Jupiter's moon Io the most
  volcanic world in the Solar System.

With a giant planet that close, it would really be Earth orbiting the
planet, not the other way round. Around Jupiter at this distance, Earth
would complete an orbit in about **a day and a half**. Jupiter would race
through its phases in our sky every 36 hours.

## Does the Moon look bigger near the horizon?

No. The Moon often looks huge when it is rising, but that is the **Moon
illusion**, a trick of perception. Its size in the sky barely changes, and
it is actually slightly smaller near the horizon, because you are then
about one Earth radius farther from it. The visualization keeps every body
at a fixed elevation and size.

## About the visualization

Each body sits at the Moon's mean distance, 384,400 km, measured centre to
centre, so its apparent size is 2 × arctan(radius ÷ distance). The camera's
field of view is fixed at 50° vertically, about what a phone camera
captures, so sizes on screen are true to life relative to the frame. Saturn's
rings run from the inner C ring to the outer edge of the A ring. The planets
are lit by a Sun that has just set behind you, and their night sides blend
into the twilight sky, as the Moon's does. For the other planets side by side
at true scale, see the [Solar System planets in a row](/posts/solar-system-row).
