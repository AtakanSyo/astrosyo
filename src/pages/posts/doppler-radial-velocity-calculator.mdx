A star's spectral lines sit at precisely known wavelengths in the lab —
Hα always at 656.28 nm, the sodium D line always at 589.0 nm. When a
star is moving toward or away from us, those lines shift: measure how
far, and you have the star's radial velocity, no other measurement
required. This is the working principle behind everything from Vesto
Slipher's first galaxy velocity measurements in the 1910s to today's
exoplanet searches, which detect worlds by the meters-per-second wobble
they induce in their host star's spectrum.

## The classical approximation

For velocities much smaller than light, the relation is a simple ratio:

$$
\frac{v_r}{c} \approx \frac{\lambda_{\rm obs} - \lambda_0}{\lambda_0}
$$

A positive shift (<Katex tex="\lambda_{\rm obs}" /> > <Katex tex="\lambda_0" />, redshifted) means the source is receding;
negative (blueshifted) means it's approaching. Take the worked example
this calculator opens with: Hα's rest wavelength is 656.28 nm; observed
at 656.50 nm, that's a radial velocity of about +100.5 km/s — receding.

## Where the exact relativistic relation is needed

The classical formula is a first-order approximation. For purely radial
motion, the exact relativistic Doppler relation is

$$
\frac{\lambda_{\rm obs}}{\lambda_0} = \sqrt{\frac{1+\beta}{1-\beta}}, \qquad \beta = \frac{v_r}{c}
$$

which already folds in relativistic time dilation, not just the
classical light-travel-time effect. At everyday stellar velocities (tens
to hundreds of km/s, <Katex tex="\beta" /> ~ 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻³) the two formulas are
indistinguishable. They start to diverge visibly only once a source is
moving at a real fraction of the speed of light — relativistic jets,
some supernova ejecta, and (going the other way) precision tests of
special relativity itself are where the distinction actually earns its
keep.

## Running it in reverse

Enter a rest wavelength and a radial velocity instead, and the
calculator predicts the observed wavelength — useful for planning an
observation (where should this line actually land, given the target's
known velocity?) or for building intuition about how large a shift a
given velocity really produces before you go looking for it in real
data.

## Reading the two charts

- **The radial velocity gauge** places the current velocity on a scale
  that auto-fits to whatever you enter, colored blue for approaching and
  red for receding. A shaded zone appears only when |v| exceeds 0.1c —
  the point past which relativistic corrections exceed about 0.5% — so
  its *absence* is itself informative: for ordinary stellar and
  exoplanet work, you'll essentially never see it.
- **The classical-vs-relativistic curve** plots both formulas across the
  full range of <Katex tex="\beta" />, dashed for classical and solid for relativistic. They
  sit on top of each other near <Katex tex="\beta = 0" /> and peel apart as |v| grows — a
  direct visual answer to "how much does the approximation actually cost
  me here," rather than a rule of thumb taken on faith.
