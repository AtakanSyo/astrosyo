A neutron star is what is left when a massive star explodes as a supernova
and its core collapses. It holds more mass than the Sun in a ball only about
24 km across, about the size of a city. The visualization above puts that
size next to familiar things, all at true scale: Mount Everest, a black
hole, the Moon, a white dwarf, Earth and the Sun. Step through the objects,
or press play for the full tour.

## How big is a neutron star?

A typical neutron star has a **radius of about 12 km**, so it is roughly
**24 km across**, a little more than the length of Manhattan (21.6 km).
Mount Everest, 8.8 km tall, would reach only a third of the way up its side.

That size is now measured, not just predicted. NASA's NICER X-ray telescope
on the International Space Station has mapped hot spots on spinning neutron
stars. How strongly gravity bends their light shows how compact the stars
are. Two pulsars give radii of about 12.7 km (PSR J0030+0451, about 1.4 solar
masses) and 12.4 km (PSR J0740+6620, about 2.1 solar masses). Most estimates
for neutron stars fall between about 11 and 14 km.

Oddly, a heavier neutron star is not much bigger, and can even be slightly
smaller: more mass squeezes the matter harder.

## How dense is a neutron star?

Packing 1.4 times the Sun's mass into a 12 km ball gives:

- **Density:** about 4 × 10¹⁷ kg per cubic metre, around the density of an
  atomic nucleus. **A teaspoon would weigh about 2 billion tonnes**, more
  than all the people on Earth put together.
- **Surface gravity:** about 130 billion times Earth's. Even without the
  extra boost relativity gives, a mountain on its surface would be at most
  millimetres tall.
- **Escape speed:** about 60% of the speed of light. A little more
  compression and nothing could escape at all: it would be a black hole.

## Neutron star vs black hole size

You might expect a black hole to be smaller than a neutron star, and for
the same mass it is: a black hole with 1.4 times the Sun's mass would have
an event horizon only about **4 km** in radius. But the black holes left by
collapsing stars are usually much heavier, and a black hole's size grows
with its mass. **Cygnus X-1**, the first black hole ever identified, has
about 21 times the Sun's mass and an event horizon about **63 km** in
radius, five times the neutron star's.

## Neutron star vs white dwarf

Stars too light to leave a neutron star end as **white dwarfs**. **Sirius B**,
the companion of the brightest star in the night sky, holds about the
Sun's mass in a ball about 5,600 km in radius, a bit smaller than Earth.
That makes it almost 500 times wider than a neutron star with a similar
mass. In a white dwarf, electrons resist the squeeze. In a neutron star,
gravity wins that fight: electrons and protons merge into neutrons, which
pack almost as tightly as matter can.

## Neutron star compared to Earth and the Sun

**Earth** is about 530 times wider than a neutron star, yet a neutron star
holds about **470,000 times Earth's mass**. The **Sun** is about 58,000
times wider, but has less mass. At the scale of the Sun, the neutron star
is far smaller than a pixel.

## Why does it have beams?

Many neutron stars are **pulsars**. They have enormous magnetic fields, and
they shine two beams of radio waves (and sometimes X-rays and gamma rays)
out of their magnetic poles. The magnetic axis is tilted from the spin axis,
so the beams sweep around like a lighthouse. If they cross Earth, we see a
pulse every turn. The visualization shows this slowed down a great deal:
newborn pulsars spin dozens of times a second, and the fastest known,
PSR J1748−2446ad, spins 716 times a second.

## About the visualization

Every object is drawn at its true radius: the neutron star at 12 km, black
holes at their event horizon (the Schwarzschild radius, as in the
[Schwarzschild Radius Calculator](/posts/schwarzschild-radius-calculator)).
Each step zooms out to the next object, and the smaller ones stay as
outlines so you can see them inside. The black hole bends the starlight
behind it. The pulsar's beams and field lines show its shape, not their
true length. For the other end of the scale, see the
[TON 618 size comparison](/posts/ton-618-size-comparison), which goes from
the Sun to the largest black holes known.
