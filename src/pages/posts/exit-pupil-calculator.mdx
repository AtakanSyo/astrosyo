Look at the eyepiece of a telescope pointed at a bright sky from a
little way back and you'll see a small bright disc floating just
behind the glass. That disc is the exit pupil: the beam of light the
telescope delivers to your eye. Its size, more than magnification,
decides how bright and how detailed a view is, and it's the most
useful number to plan an eyepiece set around.

## The formula

$$
\text{exit pupil} = \frac{D}{M} = \frac{f}{N}
$$

<Katex tex="D" /> is the aperture and <Katex tex="M" /> the
magnification. The second form is often handier: <Katex tex="f" /> is
the eyepiece's focal length and <Katex tex="N" /> the telescope's
focal ratio. A 25 mm eyepiece in an f/5.9 Dobsonian gives
<Katex tex="25 / 5.9 \approx 4.2" /> mm, whatever the size of the
telescope. Turned around, the eyepiece for a given exit pupil is
<Katex tex="f = \text{exit pupil} \times N" />, which is how the table
under the calculator is built.

## Your eye sets the upper limit

A dark-adapted pupil opens to about 7 mm in young adults and less with
age. A rough rule is half a millimetre less per decade after twenty,
so around 5 mm at sixty, but individuals vary by a millimetre or more
either way. If the exit pupil is wider than your pupil, your eye only
takes in the middle of the beam and the rest of the aperture goes to
waste: an 8 mm exit pupil seen by a 6 mm pupil turns a 200 mm scope
into a 150 mm one. The calculator estimates your pupil from your age;
if you know your own measurement, enter it instead.

That's also where the lowest useful magnification comes from:
<Katex tex="D / d_{\text{pupil}}" />, about 29× for an 8" scope and a
7 mm pupil.

## What each exit pupil is good for

The bands below are the usual observing-guide advice, rounded:

- **5–7 mm:** the widest fields. Large nebulae, star clouds, the Milky
  Way, and nebula filters, which dim the view and need all the light
  they can get.
- **2.5–5 mm:** most deep-sky objects. Enough magnification to darken
  the background sky while keeping galaxies and clusters bright.
- **1–2.5 mm:** the Moon, planets, small planetary nebulae and
  globular clusters.
- **0.5–1 mm:** close double stars and planetary detail on nights of
  very steady air.
- **Below 0.5 mm:** empty magnification. The image gets dimmer and
  softer without showing more, and floaters in your eye start to
  get in the way.

A basic set for any scope is three eyepieces at roughly 5, 2.5 and 1
mm exit pupil.
