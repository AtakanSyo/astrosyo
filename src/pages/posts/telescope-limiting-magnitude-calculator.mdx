A telescope's main job is collecting light, and the limiting magnitude
is the simplest way to put a number on how well it does it: the
faintest star you can expect to see through it. Each step of one
magnitude is a factor of about 2.5 in brightness, so the difference
between a magnitude 12 and a magnitude 14 telescope is a factor of six
in what reaches your eye.

## Light grasp

A telescope collects light over its whole aperture, while your eye
collects it through a pupil a few millimetres wide. The ratio is the
light grasp:

$$
\text{light grasp} = \left(\frac{D}{d_{\text{pupil}}}\right)^2
$$

An 8" (203 mm) scope against a 7 mm pupil collects about 840 times
more light than your eye. In magnitudes that's
<Katex tex="5\log_{10}(203/7) \approx 7.3" />.

## Sky-adjusted limit

Add that gain to the faintest star you can see with the naked eye:

$$
m = m_{\text{eye}} + 5\log_{10}\left(\frac{D}{d_{\text{pupil}}}\right)
$$

The naked-eye limit, <Katex tex="m_{\text{eye}}" />, is where the sky
comes in. Under a Bortle 4 rural/suburban sky it's around 6.3; from a
city it may be 4.5 or worse; from a truly dark site, 7.5 or better.
Pick your Bortle class from the dropdown, or if you've counted stars
in a known patch of sky and know your own limit, enter it directly.

## The classic rule

Many references give a single formula with no sky term:

$$
m = 2.5 + 5\log_{10}(D_{\text{mm}})
$$

often written as <Katex tex="7.5 + 5\log_{10}(D_{\text{cm}})" />,
which is the same thing. It amounts to assuming a naked-eye limit of
about 6.7 with a 7 mm pupil, so it fits a reasonably dark sky and
overestimates what you'll see from a town. The calculator shows it
for comparison.

## Why real limits vary

Both formulas are estimates, and observers routinely beat or fall
short of them by a magnitude:

- **Magnification helps.** Higher power darkens the sky background
  more than it dims a star, so the faintest stars show up at
  100–200×, not at the lowest power.
- **Averted vision** (looking slightly to the side of a target) uses
  the more sensitive parts of the retina and gains about half a
  magnitude.
- **Experience counts.** Practised observers go noticeably deeper than
  beginners with the same scope.
- **Seeing, transparency and optics** take some back: haze, a dewy
  or dusty mirror, or a large central obstruction all cost light.

## Point sources only

The limit applies to stars and other point-like objects: planets, the
moons of Saturn and Neptune, quasars, Pluto. A galaxy listed at
magnitude 12 spreads that light over an area of sky, so its surface
brightness is far lower and it can be invisible in a telescope whose
stellar limit is 13 or 14. For extended objects, a larger exit pupil
and a darker sky matter more than the number here; the
[Exit Pupil Calculator](/posts/exit-pupil-calculator) helps with that.
For the smallest detail a telescope can resolve rather than the
faintest light it can catch, see the
