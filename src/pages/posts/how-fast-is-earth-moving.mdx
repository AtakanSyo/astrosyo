Sitting still is an illusion. Right now you are spinning with Earth,
orbiting the Sun, circling the centre of the Milky Way with the whole Solar
System, and drifting through the universe with the galaxy. The
visualization above takes these motions one at a time. The table under it
counts how far each one has carried you since you opened this page.

## How fast is Earth moving? The short answer

| Motion | Speed | km/h | mph |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Spinning on its axis (at the equator) | 0.47 km/s | 1,670 | 1,040 |
| Orbiting the Sun | 29.8 km/s | 107,200 | 66,600 |
| Circling the Milky Way, with the Sun | about 250 km/s | about 900,000 | about 555,000 |
| The Milky Way, against the cosmic background | about 560 km/s | about 2 million | about 1.3 million |

These are four different motions, each measured against something
different, so they don't simply add up. More on that
[below](#so-how-fast-are-we-really-moving).

## How fast is Earth spinning?

Earth turns once every 23 hours 56 minutes. At the equator, the ground
covers Earth's full 40,075 km circumference in that time: about
**1,670 km/h (1,040 mph)**. Away from the equator, you travel a smaller
circle in the same time, so you move slower: about 1,280 km/h at 40°
latitude (New York, Madrid, Beijing), 840 km/h at 60° (Oslo, Anchorage) and
zero at the poles. Set your own latitude with the slider in the first step.

## How fast does Earth orbit the Sun?

Earth travels about 940 million km around the Sun each year, an average of
**29.8 km/s, or 107,200 km/h (66,600 mph)**. That is about 30 times faster
than a rifle bullet. Its orbit is slightly oval, so the speed changes a
little: 30.3 km/s in early January, when Earth is closest to the Sun, and
29.3 km/s in early July, when it is farthest.

## How fast is the Solar System moving through the galaxy?

The Sun, carrying Earth and the other planets, orbits the centre of the
Milky Way at about **250 km/s, or roughly 900,000 km/h (555,000 mph)**.
The figure comes from tracking the apparent motion of the black hole at
the galaxy's centre, Sagittarius A*, which mirrors our own motion. Even at
that speed, one lap around the galaxy takes about **200 million years**.
The last time the Sun was where it is now, dinosaurs were just starting
to dominate life on land.

The third step of the visualization shows this motion **at true scale**,
and it looks different from the popular "vortex" animations. In one year
the Sun moves about 52 times the distance from Earth to the Sun, so each
planet's path stretches into a long, gentle spiral. The planets don't
trail behind the Sun like a comet's tail. Their orbits are also tilted
about 60° to the direction of travel. You'll often see 230 km/s quoted;
recent measurements put the Sun's speed closer to 248 km/s.

## How fast is the Milky Way moving?

To measure the speed of a whole galaxy, you need something to measure it
against. The best choice is the **cosmic microwave background**, the
afterglow of the Big Bang that fills the universe. Moving through it makes
it look slightly hotter ahead of us and slightly cooler behind, by about
0.1%. The Planck satellite measured that the Sun is moving through it at
**370 km/s**. Taking away the Sun's own motion around the galaxy leaves the
Milky Way moving at **about 560 km/s, or 2 million km/h (1.3 million mph)**,
towards the constellation **Hydra**.

That motion comes from gravity on the largest scales. The Milky Way and
its neighbours in the Local Group, including Andromeda, are pulled towards
huge concentrations of galaxies such as the Great Attractor and the
Shapley Supercluster. The Local Group as a whole moves at about 620 km/s.

## So how fast are we really moving?

There is no single answer, because speed is always measured relative to
something. The four speeds above each use a different reference: Earth's
axis, the Sun, the galaxy's centre and the cosmic background. They also
point in different directions, so they partly cancel. The Sun's motion
around the galaxy happens to point roughly against the Milky Way's motion
through the universe.

The closest thing to an absolute answer is our speed against the cosmic
background: **about 370 km/s, or 1.3 million km/h (830,000 mph)**. Earth's
orbit adds or subtracts up to about 30 km/s over the year.

## Why can't we feel Earth moving?

You only feel changes in motion, not motion itself. In a smooth plane at
900 km/h you can pour a drink without spilling it, because you, the drink
and the plane all move together. Earth's motions are extraordinarily
smooth, and their changes in direction are gradual. Its orbit bends by
less than 1° a day, and its spin pushes you outwards with a force of only
about 0.3% of gravity at the equator. That makes you about 0.3% lighter
there than at the poles.

## About the visualization

- **Spin:** Earth turning once every 20 seconds, with a marker at the
  latitude you choose.
- **Around the Sun:** one year in 12 seconds. Earth and the Sun are drawn
  much larger than they would be to scale.
- **Through the galaxy:** the paths of Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars and
  Jupiter as the Sun moves at about 250 km/s. The paths are at true scale;
  the Sun and planets are drawn as glowing points.
- **Through the universe:** the Milky Way moving against the cosmic
  background. The background's temperature difference is greatly
  exaggerated so you can see it.
