Andromeda, the nearest large galaxy to ours, is about 2.5 million
light-years away and closing in at roughly 110 km/s, close to 400,000 km/h.
For years the standard story was simple: in about 4 billion years the two
galaxies collide, and a few billion years later they merge into one. Newer
measurements say the future is far less certain. This simulation lets you
watch three possible outcomes, from today to 10 billion years from now.

Drag to rotate the view, use the buttons to zoom, and switch between
scenarios at any time; the timeline keeps its place, so you can compare the
same moment in each future.

## Will the Milky Way and Andromeda collide?

**Maybe.** Andromeda is definitely heading towards us; its light is shifted
towards blue by its approach. What decides the outcome is how fast it is
also drifting sideways, and that motion is very hard to measure from 2.5
million light-years away.

- **2012:** Hubble measurements suggested almost no sideways motion, so the
  galaxies would meet nearly head-on in about 4 billion years and merge
  about 2 billion years after that.
- **2019:** Gaia data pointed to more sideways motion, which pushes the
  first encounter later and makes it less direct.
- **2025:** A study combining Hubble and Gaia data, led by Till Sawala and
  published in *Nature Astronomy*, ran thousands of simulations across the
  measurement uncertainties. It found **roughly even odds of a merger within
  the next 10 billion years**. Part of the change comes from including the
  pull of the Large Magellanic Cloud, a satellite galaxy of the Milky Way.

The three scenarios in the simulation are illustrative paths across that
range of possibilities, not predictions:

- **Direct hit:** a nearly head-on encounter, as the 2012 measurements
  suggested. A first pass in a bit over 4 billion years, then a merger about
  a billion years later.
- **Close pass, later merger:** a wider first pass that throws out long
  tidal tails, a long swing back out, and a merger nearer 8 billion years.
- **Near miss:** a faster sideways drift. The galaxies pass hundreds of
  thousands of light-years apart and don't merge within 10 billion years.

## When will Andromeda collide with the Milky Way?

If it happens, the first close encounter is most likely **4 to 5 billion
years from now**. That part of the timeline is fairly robust, because it
depends mostly on Andromeda's well-measured approach speed. Whether that
first encounter is a direct hit, a glancing pass or a miss, and whether a
merger follows, is what remains uncertain.

If the galaxies do merge, the result will be a single large, roughly
elliptical galaxy, sometimes nicknamed **Milkomeda**. In the direct-hit and
close-pass scenarios you can watch the two spiral disks being torn into
tails and arcs, then settling into one round, featureless swarm of stars.

## What happens to the Sun and Earth?

Almost certainly **nothing violent**. Galaxies are mostly empty space: the
nearest star to the Sun is more than 4 light-years away, while the Sun is
less than 5 light-seconds across. When two galaxies collide, their stars
pass between each other, and direct star-to-star collisions are vanishingly
rare. What changes is the Sun's orbit. In many simulations, including the
ones here, the Sun is flung to a different distance from the new galactic
centre, often farther out. The panel under the simulation tracks the Sun's
distance from the centre of mass as time runs.

The timing has a twist, though. In about 5 billion years the Sun itself will
swell into a red giant, and Earth will become uninhabitable long before that
as the Sun slowly brightens. So no one on Earth is likely to watch the
collision.

## What will the night sky look like?

Today Andromeda spans about 3° of the sky, roughly six full Moons, although
only its bright core is easy to see without a telescope. As the galaxies
approach it would grow across the sky, and around the first encounter it
would fill a large part of it, crossing the band of the Milky Way. After a
merger, the band of our own disk would give way to the diffuse glow of the
merged galaxy.

## How this simulation works

A full simulation of colliding galaxies, where every star pulls on every
other star, takes a supercomputer. This one uses a much cheaper method,
the one Alar and Juri Toomre used in 1972 to show how tidal tails form:

- **Only the two galaxy cores have gravity.** Each galaxy is modelled as a
  massive halo of dark matter (a Hernquist profile), which holds most of a
  galaxy's mass. The Milky Way is set to 1.0 trillion and Andromeda to 1.3
  trillion solar masses, within the published range. The two halos pull on
  each other, and **dynamical friction**, the drag a moving mass feels as it
  plows through the other halo, lets them lose energy and merge.
- **The stars are test particles.** Each one orbits in the combined pull of
  both halos but does not pull on the other stars. That lets about 100,000
  of them (about 33,000 on phones) be moved in parallel on your graphics
  card, at full frame rate.
- **The starting conditions** are today's real values: Andromeda about 785
  kiloparsecs away and approaching at 110 km/s. Only the uncertain
  sideways speed differs between the scenarios.

It is a model, not a research simulation. It leaves out gas and new star
formation, the two galaxies' disk orientations are illustrative, and the
merged galaxy's final shape is approximate. What it does capture is the
part that matters here: how the galaxies' orbits and tidal forces shape
the encounter, and how much the outcome depends on a sideways speed of a
few dozen kilometres per second.
