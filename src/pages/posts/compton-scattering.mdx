When light meets a free electron, the two can trade energy. Which way the
energy flows depends on who is moving. A high-energy photon hitting an
electron at rest loses some of its energy and comes away with a longer
wavelength: that is Compton scattering. A very fast electron hitting a
low-energy photon gives energy away, and the photon comes out hundreds or
millions of times more energetic: that is inverse Compton scattering. The
first proved that light comes in particles. The second makes many of the
X-rays and gamma rays in the universe.

## Compton scattering

In 1923 Arthur Compton fired X-rays from a molybdenum target at a block of
graphite and measured what came out at different angles. The scattered
X-rays had slightly longer wavelengths than the ones that went in, and the
shift grew with the angle. A wave picture of light can't explain that. A
particle picture can: treat the X-ray as a photon carrying momentum, let it
collide with an electron like a billiard ball, conserve energy and momentum,
and the shift comes out exactly:

$$
\lambda' - \lambda = \frac{h}{m_e c}\,(1 - \cos\theta)
$$

<Katex tex="\theta" /> is the angle the photon is deflected through, and
<Katex tex="h / m_e c" /> is a fixed length, the Compton wavelength of the
electron: 2.426 picometres. The shift doesn't depend on the photon's energy
at all. What changes is how much that shift matters. For Compton's 17.5 keV
X-rays, with a wavelength of about 71 pm, even the largest possible shift
(4.85 pm, for a photon bounced straight back) is a 7% change. For a gamma
ray the same few picometres can be most of its wavelength, and a
backscattered gamma ray hands most of its energy to the electron. Compton
won the 1927 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

In energy terms:

$$
E' = \frac{E}{1 + \frac{E}{m_e c^2}(1 - \cos\theta)}
$$

The electron recoils at an angle <Katex tex="\varphi" /> on the other side
of the original direction, carrying the energy the photon lost. The
animation shows both, and the plot below it shows how likely each angle
is, from the Klein–Nishina formula. Low-energy photons scatter forwards and
backwards about equally; high-energy photons mostly carry on forwards.

Compton scattering is not just history. It is the main way gamma rays lose
energy in matter, so it shapes every gamma-ray detector's spectrum: the
sharp "Compton edge" is the most energy a photon can leave behind in one
collision (477 keV for the 662 keV gamma rays from caesium-137). It also
blurs medical X-ray images, which is why X-ray machines use anti-scatter
grids.

## Inverse Compton scattering

Now run the collision the other way. Let the electron move at nearly the
speed of light, with Lorentz factor <Katex tex="\gamma" />, and let it meet a
low-energy photon: a microwave photon left over from the Big Bang, say, or
a photon of starlight. In the electron's own frame the photon arrives
blueshifted by a factor of about <Katex tex="\gamma" />. It scatters, and
back in our frame it is blueshifted again by about <Katex tex="\gamma" />.
Averaged over directions:

$$
\langle E_1 \rangle \approx \frac{4}{3}\,\gamma^2 E
$$

The square is what makes this so powerful. An electron with
<Katex tex="\gamma = 10{,}000" /> (an energy of about 5 GeV) turns a
cosmic-microwave-background photon of 0.0006 eV into an X-ray of about
84 keV, a gain of a hundred million. The scattered photons are also beamed
forwards, into a narrow cone of half-angle about <Katex tex="1/\gamma" />
around the electron's direction of travel: the animation shows the cone.

There is a limit. When <Katex tex="\gamma E" /> approaches the electron's
rest energy (511 keV), the electron would be handing over a large share of
its own energy in one go, and the simple <Katex tex="\gamma^2" /> rule
breaks down. In this Klein–Nishina regime the scattered photon can carry at
most about the electron's whole energy, <Katex tex="\gamma m_e c^2" />.

### Where it happens

- **The Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect.** The hot gas in a galaxy cluster
  (around 100 million kelvin) is full of electrons moving at a few percent
  of light speed. They nudge some of the cosmic microwave background
  photons passing through to slightly higher energies, which leaves a
  faint dip in the microwave sky behind every big cluster. Surveys use it
  to find clusters at any distance.
- **Blazars and quasars.** Jets of electrons near the speed of light
  scatter the light of the surrounding gas and dust up to gamma-ray
  energies.
- **The Crab Nebula.** The same electrons that make the nebula's radio to
  X-ray glow by synchrotron radiation scatter some of those photons up to
  TeV gamma rays, a process called synchrotron self-Compton.
- **Radio galaxy lobes.** Electrons in giant lobes scatter the cosmic
  microwave background into X-rays, which is how X-ray telescopes see lobes
  far beyond the visible galaxy.

Inverse Compton scattering also works as a brake. Every scattering takes
energy from the electron, so a fast electron in a dense bath of photons
cools quickly. That puts a ceiling on how bright compact radio sources can
appear, at a brightness temperature of about a trillion kelvin.
